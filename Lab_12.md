## My Eli Lilly Analysis

### Target Selection
I chose Eli Lilly because I was interested in the pharmaceutical industry and wanted to analyze a company with major growth from products like Mounjaro and Zepbound.

### Company and Evidence
Eli Lilly makes money primarily by developing and selling pharmaceutical products. Two important products behind its recent growth are Mounjaro and Zepbound.

For my analysis, I used Lilly's SEC filings and financial statements. Revenue increased from $34.124 billion in 2023 to $45.043 billion in 2024 and $65.179 billion in 2025. I used this historical financial data as the starting point for my forecast assumptions.

### Pro-Forma
I used Lilly's historical results to create forecast assumptions for 2026-2030. My base revenue growth path was 32%, 20%, 15%, 12%, and 10%, with an 83% gross margin.

My company-specific focus was Lilly's R&D spending because pharmaceutical companies depend heavily on research and drug development. My full linked FCFE valuation remained unresolved, so I did not create a value that the model could not support.

### Valuation
My DCF valuation estimated Lilly at about $371.59 per share, compared with a market price of about $1,124.21 when I did the analysis.

That suggested Lilly was overvalued relative to my base-case DCF assumptions. My conclusion was not that Lilly is a weak company, but that the market price reflected much stronger expectations than my model.

My peer-comparison valuation was much less stable, so I treated it as less reliable than the DCF.

### Sensitivity and Drivers
I tested two operating drivers from my model: revenue growth and gross margin.

For revenue growth, I shifted my 2026-2030 base growth path by plus or minus 5 percentage points. The base path was 32%, 20%, 15%, 12%, and 10%.

For gross margin, I tested 81%, 83%, and 85%.

Over these ranges, revenue growth had the larger effect on 2030 operating profit. The revenue-growth scenarios produced a $28.49 billion operating-profit span, compared with a $4.65 billion span for gross margin.

This does not mean revenue growth is always more important. The ranking depends partly on the ranges I chose.

### Interpretation
My current conclusion is that Lilly appeared overvalued relative to my DCF assumptions. My estimated value was about $371.59 per share compared with the $1,124.21 market price I used.

I would keep my current conclusion for now, but I would investigate Lilly's long-term revenue growth assumptions further. Evidence that Lilly can sustain substantially higher growth than my base forecast could change my valuation conclusion.

My view changed from initially focusing on Lilly's strong business growth to realizing that a strong company can still appear expensive if the market price already reflects very high expectations.
